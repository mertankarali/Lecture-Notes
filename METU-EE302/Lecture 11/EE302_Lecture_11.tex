\section{Stability}

A SISO system is called BIBO (bounded-input--bounded-output) stable,
if the output will be bounded for \textbf{every} input to the system that is
bounded. 

  \begin{minipage}[h]{1\linewidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{sys}
    \end{center}
  \end{minipage}
 
If we use the impulse response representation for an LTI SISO system, i.e. $y(t) =
\int_{0}^{t} g(\tau) u(t - \tau) d \tau$ (we assume that system is causal),
the system is BIBO stable if and only if its impulse response is 
absolutely integrable.
%
\begin{align*}
   \mathrm{BIBO} \ \mathrm{Stable} \ \Leftrightarrow \ \int_0^{\infty } |
  g(t) | dt < B < \infty
\end{align*}
 %
However, we generally use transfer function representation in this
course. Let 
%
\begin{align*}
  \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} =  G(s) = \frac{N(s)}{D(s)}
\end{align*}
%
In this context, a rational transfer function representation is BIBO stable if and only if, 
 the poles of $G(s)$ (or roots of $D(s)$ ) are strictly located  in
 the open left half $s-$plane.

\ex Show that $G(s) = \frac{1}{s}$ is not BIBO stable

\sol Let's first check the impulse response condition
%
\begin{align*}
  g(t) &= 1 , \ t \geq 0
  \\
  \lim_{t \to \infty} \int_{0}^{t} | g(\tau) | d \tau &= \lim_{t \to \infty}
  \int_{0}^{t} 1 d \tau = \infty 
\end{align*}
%
Thus the system is BIBO unstable. The system has a single pole at the
origin, so we already know that it is BIBO unstable. Now, let's 
find a specific bounded input such that output is unbounded.
Let $u(t)$ be the unit-step input then
%
\begin{align*}
   Y(s) &= \frac{1}{s^2} \ \Rightarrow \ y(t) = t  \ , t \geq 0
\\
 \lim_{t \to \infty} | y(t) | &= \infty
\end{align*}
%
We obtained an unbounded output for a bounded input.

\ex Let $G(s) = \frac{1}{s^2 + 1}$. Find a
bounded input, such that output is unbounded.
%
\sol Let $u(t) = \cos t \ , t \geq 0$, then
%
\begin{align*}
  Y(s) &= G(s) U(s) = \frac{s}{(s^2+1)^2}
  \\
  y(t) &= t \sin t \ , t \geq 0
\end{align*}
%
The output grows without bound because the input frequency equals the frequency of the poles at $\pm j$ (resonance). 

\subsection{Stability of First \& Second Order Systems}

In order to gain some intuition about how to check stability
of general rational LTI systems, we will analyze first and second order
systems. 

The transfer function of a first order system has the form
%
\begin{align*}
  D(s) &= a_0 s + a_1 \\
 a_0 &> 0 , \ \text{(w.l.o.g.)}
\end{align*}
%
The single pole of the system and associated stability condition 
can be derived as
%
\begin{align*}
  p &= -\frac{a_1}{a_0}
 \\
  \mathrm{Stable} &\Leftrightarrow a_1 > 0
\end{align*}

Now let's analyze second order systems. Transfer function
of a second order system has the following form
%
\begin{align*}
  D(s) &= a_0 s^2 + a_1 s + a_2\\
 a_0 &> 0 , \ \text{(w.l.o.g.)}
\end{align*}
%
Since $a_0 > 0$, $D(s) \to +\infty$ as $s \to \pm\infty$ on the real axis. Now look at
$D(0) = a_2$:
%
\begin{itemize}
     \item If $D(0) = a_2 < 0$, $D(s)$ changes sign on both the negative and the positive real
       axis: one pole is in the open left half-plane and the other one is in the open right
       half-plane.
     \item If $D(0) = a_2 = 0$, there is a pole at the origin.
\end{itemize}
%
In this context, we can derive the first condition (necessary but not
sufficient) 
%
\begin{align*}
 \mathrm{Stable} \Rightarrow a_2 > 0 
\end{align*}
%
Under this condition, we can re-write the characteristic
equation in a more standard form
%
\begin{align*}
  &D(s) = a_0 \left( s^2 + \frac{a_1}{a_0} s + \frac{a_2}{a_0} \right)
  \\
  &D(s) = a_0 \left( s^2 + 2 \zeta \omega_n s + \omega_n^2 \right)
  \\
  &\mathrm{where}
\\
 &\omega_n = \sqrt{a_2 / a_0} > 0 
\\
  &\zeta = \frac{a_1}{2 a_0 \omega_n} 
\end{align*}
%
From the analysis of second order system in standard form,
we know that when 
%
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\zeta < 0$, system poles are located in the open right-half
    plane
  \item $\zeta = 0$, system poles are located on the imaginary axis
  \item $\zeta > 0$, system poles are located in the open left-half
    plane
\end{itemize}
%
It is also easy to see that $\zeta > 0 \Leftrightarrow a_1 > 0$. 
As a result, we can derive a necessary and sufficient condition on
BIBO stability. 

A second order system with $D(s) = a_0 s^2 + a_1 s + a_2$ (with $a_0 >
0$), is BIBO stable if and only if $a_1 > 0$ and $a_2 > 0$. For first and second order
systems, all coefficients positive is necessary \emph{and} sufficient. From third order on it
is only necessary; the Routh test below gives the full answer.

\subsection{Routh's Stability Criterion} 

Routh's stability criterion (also called the Routh--Hurwitz criterion) is a necessary and
sufficient test for the stability of an LTI system. It decides whether all roots of $D(s)$ are
in the open left half-plane \emph{without} computing the roots: it uses only the coefficients,
with a few multiplications and divisions.

In this test, one first constructs the Routh table for a given characteristic polynomial
%
\begin{align*}
D(s) = a_0 s^n + a_1 s^{n-1} + a_2 s^{n-2} + \cdots + a_{n-2} s^{2}
+ a_{n-1} s^{1} + a_n 
\end{align*}  
%
which has $n+1$ rows. As before we take $a_0 > 0$. The first two rows hold the coefficients
(\RA{blue}), alternately; each further row is computed from the two rows above it (\RC{orange}).
Missing entries are taken as $0$. The first column (shaded) is the one used in the test.
%
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{|c || F || c c c c |}
\hline
$s^n$ & $\RA{a_0}$ & $\RA{a_2}$ & $\RA{a_4}$ & $\RA{a_6}$ & $\cdots$
\\ \hline
$s^{n-1}$ & $\RA{a_1}$ & $\RA{a_3}$ & $\RA{a_5}$ & $\RA{a_7}$ & $\cdots$
\\ \hline
$s^{n-2}$ & $\RC{b_1}$ & $\RC{b_2}$ & $\RC{b_3}$ & $\RC{b_4}$ & $\cdots$
\\ \hline
$s^{n-3}$ & $\RC{c_1}$ & $\RC{c_2}$ & $\RC{c_3}$ & $\RC{c_4}$ & $\cdots$
\\ \hline
$s^{n-4}$ & $\RC{d_1}$ & $\RC{d_2}$ & $\RC{d_3}$ & $\RC{d_4}$ & $\cdots$
\\ \hline
$\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & & &
\\ \hline
$s^{2}$ & $\RC{\ast}$ & $\RC{\ast}$ & & &
\\ \hline
$s^{1}$ & $\RC{\ast}$ & & & &
\\ \hline
$s^{0}$ & $\RC{\ast}$ & & & &
\\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

All computed entries follow one rule:

\]
\end{rulebox}

\smallskip
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{Determinant (numerator):} take the two rows just above. Use their
        \textcolor{rsel}{first column} together with \textcolor{rsel}{column $j+1$}. This gives a
        $2 \times 2$ matrix; put a minus sign in front of its determinant.
  \item \textbf{Denominator:} the \textcolor{rpiv}{pivot}, the first entry of the row just above.
        It is the same for the whole row.
  \item A missing entry is taken as $0$.
\end{itemize}

Applied to the first rows, the rule gives


the coefficients $b_i$ (pivot $a_1$):

\begin{align*}
 b_1 = - \mathrm{det}\left( \left[ \begin{array}{cc} a_0 & a_2 \\ a_1

                                                           \right]
                                                           \right) / a_1

\frac{a_1 a_2 - a_0 a_3 }{a_1}

                                                           a_5 \end{array}
                                                           \right]
                                                           \right) / a_1
=
\frac{a_1 a_4 - a_0 a_5 }{a_1}
\\
b_3 = - \mathrm{det}\left( \left[ \begin{array}{cc} a_0 & a_6 \\ a_1
                                                         &
                                                           a_7 \end{array}
                                                           \right]
                                                           \right) / a_1
=
\frac{a_1 a_6 - a_0 a_7 }{a_1}
\\
\vdots
\end{align*}

%
%
\begin{align*}

                                                         &

                                                           \right]

%
\begin{align*}

                                      c_1 & c_2 \end{array}
                                                           \right]
                                                           \right) /c_1

\frac{c_1 b_2 - b_1 c_2 }{c_1}
\\

                                                         &

\\

\end{align*}  
%

\vspace{6pt}

\needspace{10\baselineskip}
\begin{rulebox}{Reading the Routh table ($a_0 > 0$)}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Result 1 (necessary condition):} if any coefficient $a_i \leq 0$, the system is
        not BIBO stable. All coefficients must be strictly positive; this alone is not
        sufficient.
  \item \textbf{Result 2 (necessary and sufficient):} the system is BIBO stable if and only if
        all entries in the first column are strictly positive.
  \item \textbf{Result 3:} the number of roots of $D(s)$ with positive real part equals the
        number of sign changes in the first column $a_0, a_1, b_1, c_1, d_1, \dots$
\end{itemize}
\end{rulebox}

\textbf{Procedure.} (1) Check the signs of the coefficients. (2) Build the table row by row.
(3) Count the sign changes in the first column.

\subsubsection{Routh--Hurwitz Test: Special Cases}

\paragraph{Case I: a zero in the first column.}

Let's analyze the stability of ``$D(s) = s^3 - 3 s + 2$''. 
Since $a_1 = 0$ and $a_2 = -3$, we know that the system
is not BIBO stable. Let's construct the Routh table to verify this result.

 \begin{minipage}[h]{1\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c || F || c  |}
\hline
$s^3$ & 1 & $-3$ 
\\ \hline
$s^2$ & 0 & 2
\\ \hline
$s^1$  & ? & 
\\ \hline
$s^0$  & ? & 
\\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
  \end{minipage}

We can see that one of the coefficients in the Routh array is zero,
and we cannot continue: the next row would divide by $0$. A zero in the first column already
tells us that the system is not BIBO stable. To find the number of poles with positive real
part, we have three options.

\vspace{12pt}

\textbf{Solution Type 1:} Let's find a new $\bar{D}(s) = (s + \alpha)
D(s), \ \alpha > 0$. We know that $D(s)$ and $\bar{D}(s)$ have the same 
$\#$ poles with positive real parts. Then we can construct a Routh
table for $\bar{D}(s)$ to seek an answer. 

Let $\bar{D}(s) = (s + 2) ( s^3 - 3 s + 2) = s^4 + 2 s^3 - 3 s^2 - 4 s+ 4$, the Routh table takes the form

%

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c || F || c c |}
\hline
$s^4$ & 1 & -3 & 4 
\\ \hline
$s^3$ & 2 & -4 & 0
\\ \hline
$s^2$ & -1 & 4 & 0
\\ \hline
$s^1$ & 4 & 0 &
\\ \hline
$s^0$ & 4 & 0 & 
\\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

%
$\#$ sign changes in the Routh array of $\bar{D}(s)$ is equal to 2, so
we can conclude that $D(s)$ has 2 poles with positive real parts. If
we compute the poles of $D(s)$, we find that $p_1 = -2$, $p_{2,3} = 1$,
which verifies our finding. 

\vspace{12pt}

\textbf{Solution Type 2:} Replace 0 element with an infinitesimal but
non-zero element $\epsilon >0$.
%

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c || F || c  |}
\hline
$s^3$ & 1 & $-3$ 
\\ \hline
$s^2$ & $\epsilon$ & 2
\\ \hline
$s^1$  & $- \dfrac{3 \epsilon + 2}{\epsilon}$ & 0 
\\ \hline
$s^0$  & 2 & 
\\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}


$\#$ sign changes in the perturbed Routh array of $D(s)$ is equal to 2, so
we can conclude that $D(s)$ has 2 poles with positive real parts.

\textbf{Solution Type 3:} Replace $s$ with $1/q$
%
\begin{align*}
  D(s)|_{s =1/q} = q^{-3} - 3 q^{-1} + 2 = q^{-3} 
  \left( 2 q^{3} - 3 q^2 + 1 \right)
\end{align*}
%
Now let's define $\bar{D}(q) = q^3 D(s)|_{s =1/q} =  2 q^3 - 3 q^2 +
1$. 
Note that we simply flip the coefficients of $D(s)$
to find the coefficients of $\bar{D}(q)$. It is easy to see that 
if $p_i$ is a pole of $D(s)$, then $1/p_i$ is a pole of $\bar{D}(q)$.
Let $p_i = \sigma + j \omega$, then 
%
\begin{align*}
  \frac{1}{p_i} &= \frac{1}{\sigma + j \omega} = \frac{\sigma - j
  \omega}{\sigma^2 + \omega^2}
\\
&= \frac{\sigma}{\sigma^2 + \omega^2} - j \frac{
  \omega}{\sigma^2 + \omega^2}
\\
\mathrm{Sign} ( \mathrm{Re} \lbrace p_i \rbrace ) &= 
\mathrm{Sign} \left( \mathrm{Re} \left\lbrace \frac{1}{p_i} \right\rbrace \right)
\end{align*}
%
We can see that $D(s)$ and $\bar{D}(q)$ have same number of stable
and unstable poles. Thus, we can perform a Routh Hurwitz test on
$\bar{D}(q)$
%

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c || F || c  |}
\hline
$q^3$ & 2 & 0 
\\ \hline
$q^2$ & -3 & 1
\\ \hline
$q^1$  & 2/3 & 0 
\\ \hline
$q^0$  & 1 & 
\\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}


$\#$ sign changes in the Routh array of $\bar{D}(q)$ is equal to 2, so
we can conclude that $D(s)$ has 2 poles with positive real parts.

\paragraph{Case II: a whole row of zeros.}

\ex Analyze the stability of $D(s) = s^4 + 2 s^3 + 2 s^2 + 2 s + 1$ using Routh table

\begin{minipage}[h]{1\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c || F || c c |}
\hline
$s^4$ & 1 & 2 & 1 
\\ \hline
$s^3$ & 2 & 2 & 0
\\ \hline
$s^2$ & 1 & 1 & 0
\\ \hline
$s^1$ & 0 & 0 &
\\ \hline
$s^0$ & ? & ? & 
\\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{minipage}

We can see that one row in Routh table is completely zero. 
Thus we know that system is indeed BIBO unstable. However,
what can we do in order to find the number of unstable poles? 
This happens when there exist roots of equal magnitude
located radially opposite in $s-$plane, i.e. symmetric
w.r.t. origin. These cases are illustrated in the figure below.

\vspace{6pt}

\begin{minipage}[h]{1\linewidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{caseII}
    \end{center}

\vspace{6pt}

The row above the zero row (here the $s^2$ row) gives the \emph{auxiliary polynomial}; its
entries are the coefficients of every second power of $s$. Here $A(s) = s^2 + 1$ in this
example. Then we compute its derivative, $A'(s) = 2 s$ in this example,
and use the coefficient of $A'(s)$ in replacement of the zero row and compute
the Routh array accordingly. This process is illustrated below

\vspace{6pt}
\begin{minipage}[h]{1\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c || F || c c | l |}
\hline
$s^4$ & 1 & 2 & 1 &
\\ \hline
$s^3$ & 2 & 2 & 0 &
\\ \hline
$s^2$ & 1 & 1 & 0 & $\rightarrow A(s) = s^2 + 1$
\\ \hline
$s^1$ & \textbf{2} & \textbf{0} &   & $\leftarrow A'(s) = 2s$
\\ \hline
$s^0$ & 1 & 0 & &
\\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{minipage}
\vspace{6pt}

$\#$ sign changes in the Routh array of $D(s)$ with Auxiliary polynomial
is equal to 0, so we can conclude that $D(s)$ has 0 poles with
positive real parts. This means that all unstable poles are located on
the imaginary axis. If we compute the poles numerically we find that
$p_{1,2} = -1$ and $p_{3,4} = \pm j$ which verifies our finding. Indeed,
we can see that problematic roots are the roots of $A(s)$.

\subsubsection{Routh--Hurwitz Test: Relative Stability}

Sometimes absolute stability is not enough. We may want all poles to satisfy
$\mathrm{Re}\{s\} < -4$, for example to meet a settling-time requirement (the gray region below).

  \begin{minipage}[h]{1\linewidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{settling}
    \end{center}
  \end{minipage}

The Routh test still works after a change of variables that shifts the line
$\mathrm{Re}\{s\} = -4$ to the imaginary axis.

Replace $s$ with $z - 4$
%
\begin{align*}
  D(s)|_{s = z- 4} = D(z)
\end{align*}
%
If we apply a Routh Hurwitz test on $D(z)$, we compute the $\#$
poles of $D(z)$ with positive real parts. Based on change of
variables, we defined 
%
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{If} \ \mathrm{Re} \lbrace z_i \rbrace > 0 \Leftrightarrow
\mathrm{Re} \lbrace s_i \rbrace > -4
\end{align*}
%
where $z_i$ and $s_i$ are poles in associated planes. 

\vspace{12pt}

\ex Let $D(s) = s^3 + 8 s^2 + 19 s + 12$, first test if the
system is BIBO stable using Routh Hurwitz test. If the system is BIBO 
stable, then check if the real parts of all the poles are located in the
region $\sigma \in (-\infty , -2)$.

\sol First test the absolute stability using Routh test
on $D(s)$

\vspace{6pt}
\begin{minipage}[h]{1\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c || F || c  |}
\hline
$s^3$ & 1 & 19
\\ \hline
$s^2$ & 8 & 12
\\ \hline
$s^1$ & 17.5 & 0 
\\ \hline
$s^0$ & 12 & 0  
\\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{minipage}
\vspace{6pt}

Since all entries in the first column are positive, the system is BIBO stable. Now check the relative stability by applying
Routh test on  ``$D(z) = D(s)|_{s = z - 2} =  z^3 + 2 z^2 - z - 2$'',

\vspace{6pt}
\begin{minipage}[h]{1\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c || F || c | l |}
\hline
$z^3$ & 1 & -1 & 
\\ \hline
$z^2$ & 2 & -2 & $\rightarrow A(z) = 2 z^2 - 2$
\\ \hline
$z^1$ & 4 & 0 & $\leftarrow A'(z) = 4 z $
\\ \hline
$z^0$ & -2 & 0 &
\\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{minipage}
\vspace{6pt}

Since in the Routh table (with Auxiliary polynomial), there exist one
sign change, $D(z)$ has one pole in the open right half z-plane.
This means that $D(s)$ has a single pole where its real part is 
located in the $\sigma \in (-2 , 0)$ region.

If we compute the roots of $D(s)$ numerically, we find that
$p_1 = -4$, $p_2 = -3$, and $p_3 = -1$. This verifies that
the system is BIBO stable but, only two of the poles are located
in the $\sigma \in (-\infty , -2)$ region.
