\textbf{Why this lecture?} So far, KCL and KVL were written for any circuit, with any
elements. From now on we restrict the elements. In an \textbf{LTI resistive circuit} every
equation is linear and algebraic. Such a circuit can be solved in two ways: by transforming it
into a simpler circuit, or by writing the equations of the circuit as it is. This lecture gives
the tools for the first way: the
substitution theorem, one-ports and their equivalents, series and parallel connections, and
the exchange of a voltage source and a current source.

%==============================================================
\section{LTI resistive circuits}

First classify the elements, then the circuit. Independent sources are left aside: they are
the inputs (see below).

\begin{definitionbox}{Resistive and dynamic elements}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{resistive} element relates its voltages and currents at the same instant by
        an algebraic law; it has no memory. Examples: resistors, diodes, dependent sources,
        ideal transformers, and the ideal op-amp met later. ``Resistive'' covers all of them,
        not only resistors.
  \item A \textbf{dynamic} element has a derivative in its law, so it has memory: capacitors
        ($\Icol{i} = C\,d\Vcol{v}/dt$), inductors, coupled inductors.
  \item An element is \textbf{linear} if its law is linear in its voltages and currents, and
        \textbf{time-invariant} if its law does not change with time.
\end{itemize}
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{definitionbox}{Nonlinear, time-varying, dynamic circuits}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{nonlinear} circuit contains at least one nonlinear element, e.g.\ a diode. Otherwise it is
        \textbf{linear}.
  \item A \textbf{time-varying} circuit contains at least one time-varying element, e.g.\ $R(t)$. Otherwise it is
        \textbf{time-invariant}.
  \item A \textbf{dynamic} circuit contains at least one dynamic element: an $L$ or a $C$. Otherwise it is
        \textbf{resistive}.
\end{itemize}
An \textbf{LTI resistive circuit} is linear, time-invariant and resistive: apart from the
independent sources, every element is linear, time-invariant and resistive (LTI resistors,
linear dependent sources, ideal transformers, \dots).
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{ideabox}[Independent sources are inputs]
An independent source is an \textbf{external input}: a given signal of time, such as
$\Vcol{v_s(t)} = 5\cos t\V$. The three pairs of words describe the circuit that the inputs
drive, not the inputs.
\end{ideabox}

\textbf{What varies in time?} In every circuit the voltages and currents are functions of time.
What matters is the \emph{element law}. A time-invariant resistor obeys $\Vcol{v(t)} =
R\,\Icol{i(t)}$ with the same $R$ at every instant; a time-varying one obeys $\Vcol{v(t)} =
R(t)\,\Icol{i(t)}$: the law itself changes. Linear and nonlinear also refer to the element law.

(b) is LTI resistive although its source varies in time. In (d), $R(t) = 2 + \sin t\ \Omega$.
(c) and (d) are still resistive; (e) is still linear and time-invariant.
\end{examplebox}

\textbf{What we gain.} KCL and KVL are \emph{always} linear: they come from the graph and
need no assumption on the elements. In an LTI resistive circuit every element equation is
linear and algebraic too: $v = Ri$ for a resistor, $v = \mu v_x$ or $i = g\,v_x$
for a dependent source, $v_2 = n v_1$ and $i_1 = -n i_2$ for an ideal transformer. No derivative appears. Time enters only through the values of the
independent sources, so at each instant the circuit is solved like a circuit with constant
sources.

\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.66]\exN{3}\vilab{2.85}\end{tikzpicture} &

\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.66]\exStep{3}\vilab{3.2}\end{tikzpicture}\\[-2pt]

\end{tabular}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.38\linewidth}
\raggedright
The load $R_L$ can be any resistive element. Find $\Vcol{v}$ and $\Icol{i}$.\par\medskip
Leave $R_L$ aside and look only at the part left of $a$--$b$. The $2\ohm$ has $12 - \Vcol{v}$
across it, the $6\ohm$ has $\Vcol{v}$. KCL at $a$:
\[\frac{12 - \Vcol{v}}{2} = \frac{\Vcol{v}}{6} + \Icol{i}
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Vcol{v} = 9 - 1.5\,\Icol{i}.\]
A $9\V$ source in series with $1.5\ohm$ obeys the same law (KVL), so $R_L$ cannot tell the two

\end{minipage}\par\medskip
\textbf{Any load.} With $\Vcol{v} = R_L\Icol{i}$: $\Icol{i} = 9/(1.5 + R_L)$. For
$R_L = 3\ohm$: $\Icol{i} = 2\A$, $\Vcol{v} = 6\V$.
\end{examplebox}

\textbf{What did we use?} The part left of $a$--$b$ became one source and one resistor, and
$R_L$ did not notice. The work is done once; each new load is one line of algebra. Three
questions make this precise:
\begin{itemize}
  \item May a part be replaced by a source? --- the \textbf{substitution theorem} (Section~3).
  \item When may a part be replaced by a simpler part? --- \textbf{equivalent one-ports}
        (Section~4).
  \item Which simple replacements are there? --- \textbf{series, parallel}, \textbf{source
        transformation} (Sections 5, 6).
\end{itemize}

\section{The substitution theorem}

Split a circuit into two parts $N_1$ and $N_2$, joined at two terminals $a$ and $b$. Let
$\Vcol{v(t)}$ be the voltage between $a$ and $b$, and $\Icol{i(t)}$ the current from $N_1$
into $N_2$ at $a$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c@{\hspace{6mm}}c@{\hspace{6mm}}c@{}}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8]
  \pairbox{$N_1$}{$N_2$}
\end{tikzpicture} &
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8]
  \subsbox{0}
\end{tikzpicture} &
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8]
  \subsbox{1}
\end{tikzpicture}\\[2pt]
{\small (I) the circuit} & {\small (II) $N_2$ $\to$ voltage source}
  & {\small (III) $N_2$ $\to$ current source}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{ideabox}[Substitution theorem]
Replace $N_2$ by an independent voltage source $\Vcol{v_s(t)} = \Vcol{v(t)}$, or by an
independent current source $\Icol{i_s(t)} = \Icol{i(t)}$. No branch voltage or branch current
inside $N_1$ changes. An observer inside $N_1$ cannot tell (I), (II) and (III) apart.
\end{ideabox}

\textbf{Why it works.} $N_1$ meets the rest of the circuit only at $a$ and $b$. So, seen from
inside $N_1$, the rest is nothing but a voltage $\Vcol{v}$ and a current $\Icol{i}$ at these
terminals; in (I) they take the values $\Vcol{v^*}(t)$ and $\Icol{i^*}(t)$. A voltage source
$\Vcol{v_s} = \Vcol{v^*}$ fixes the voltage and lets the current be whatever $N_1$ drives through
it, which is $\Icol{i^*}$. A current source $\Icol{i_s} = \Icol{i^*}$ fixes the current and lets
the voltage be whatever it must be, which is $\Vcol{v^*}$. In all three cases $N_1$ has the same
equations and sees the same values at $a$--$b$, so it has the same solution. $N_2$ itself never
enters the argument: it can be anything, even nonlinear.

\textbf{One condition.} The new circuit must have a unique solution. If $N_1$ is a single
$2\A$ current source and $N_2$ is replaced by a $2\A$ current source, KCL holds for every
$\Vcol{v}$: the voltage is no longer fixed.

\begin{examplebox}[Example 10.3 --- substitution in the first example]
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c@{\hspace{3mm}}c@{\hspace{3mm}}c@{}}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.68]
  \exN{0}
\end{tikzpicture} &
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.68]
  \exN{1}
\end{tikzpicture} &
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.68]
  \exN{2}
\end{tikzpicture}\\[2pt]
{\small (I) $N_2$: $R_L = 3\ohm$} & {\small (II) a $6\V$ source} & {\small (III) a $2\A$ source}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textbf{(I)} From Example 10.2 with $R_L = 3\ohm$: $\Vcol{v} = 6\V$ and $\Icol{i} = 2\A$;
inside $N_1$ the $2\ohm$ carries $(12 - 6)/2 = 3\A$ and the $6\ohm$ carries $1\A$.

\textbf{(II)} The $6\V$ source fixes $\Vcol{v} = 6\V$. Again the $2\ohm$ carries $3\A$ and the
$6\ohm$ carries $1\A$; the source takes the remaining $2\A$.

\textbf{(III)} The $2\A$ source takes $2\A$ from node $a$. KCL at $a$:
$(12 - \Vcol{v})/2 = \Vcol{v}/6 + 2$, so $\Vcol{v} = 6\V$, and the currents inside $N_1$ are
$3\A$ and $1\A$ again.
\end{examplebox}

\section{Ports and equivalent one-ports}

\begin{definitionbox}{Port, one-port}
A pair of terminals is a \textbf{port} if the current entering one terminal equals the current
leaving the other. A \textbf{one-port} is a circuit, or a circuit element, with one pair of
terminals accessible from outside.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.3\linewidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
  \oneport{$N$}
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.66\linewidth}
A one-port is always a port: by KCL on a closed surface around $N$, the current $\Icol{i}$
that enters at $a$ leaves at $b$. We take $\Icol{i}$ entering the $+$ terminal of
$\Vcol{v}$. The relation between $\Vcol{v}$ and $\Icol{i}$ is the \textbf{$i$--$v$
characteristic} of the one-port. A resistor is the simplest one-port: $\Vcol{v} = R\Icol{i}$.
\end{minipage}\par\smallskip
\end{center}

\begin{definitionbox}{Equivalent one-ports}
Two one-ports $N_1$ and $N_2$ are \textbf{equivalent}, $N_1 \equiv N_2$, if they have the
same $i$--$v$ characteristic at their terminals. Then, for any one-port $N_0$ connected to
them, every branch voltage and branch current inside $N_0$ is the same with $N_1$ or with
$N_2$.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c@{\hspace{10mm}}c@{}}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8]
  \pairbox{$N_0$}{$N_1$}
\end{tikzpicture} &
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8]
  \pairbox{$N_0$}{$N_2$}
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
$N_0$ meets the rest only through $\Vcol{v}$ and $\Icol{i}$. The equations of the whole
circuit are those of $N_0$ plus the $i$--$v$ characteristic at the port. Equivalent one-ports
give the same equations, so the same solution inside $N_0$. Inside $N_1$ and $N_2$ the
branch values may differ.

\section{Series and parallel connections}

\subsection*{Series connection}
In a series connection the same current $\Icol{i}$ flows through every element (KCL), and
the voltages add (KVL).

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c@{\hspace{2mm}}c@{\hspace{2mm}}c@{\hspace{4mm}}l@{}}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.75, baseline=(c.base)]
  \seriesR \coordinate (c) at (0,0.5);
\end{tikzpicture} & $\equiv$ &
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.75, baseline=(c.base)]
  \eqone{1.2}{R, l=$R_{eq}$} \coordinate (c) at (0,0.5);
\end{tikzpicture} &
$R_{eq} = R_1 + R_2 + \dots + R_k$\\[6pt]
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.75, baseline=(c.base)]
  \seriesV \coordinate (c) at (0,0.5);
\end{tikzpicture} & $\equiv$ &
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.75, baseline=(c.base)]
  \eqone{1.2}{american voltage source, l_=$v_{s,eq}$} \coordinate (c) at (0,0.5);
\end{tikzpicture} &
$v_{s,eq} = v_{s1} + v_{s2} + \dots + v_{sk}$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For the resistors: $\Vcol{v} = R_1\Icol{i} + \dots + R_k\Icol{i} = R_{eq}\Icol{i}$. The share
of $R_j$ is $\Vcol{v_j} = (R_j/R_{eq})\,\Vcol{v}$ (\textbf{voltage divider}). For the sources
all $+$ signs point towards $a$; a source turned the other way enters the sum with $-$.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\setlength{\parskip}{0.7ex plus .1ex}
\subsection*{Parallel connection}
In a parallel connection every element has the same voltage $\Vcol{v}$ (KVL), and the
currents add (KCL).

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c@{\hspace{2mm}}c@{\hspace{2mm}}c@{\hspace{4mm}}l@{}}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85, baseline=(c.base)]
  \parR \coordinate (c) at (0,0.65);
\end{tikzpicture} & $\equiv$ &
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85, baseline=(c.base)]
  \eqone{1.5}{R, l=$R_{eq}$} \coordinate (c) at (0,0.65);
\end{tikzpicture} &
$\dfrac{1}{R_{eq}} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} + \dots + \dfrac{1}{R_k}$\\[12pt]
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85, baseline=(c.base)]
  \parI \coordinate (c) at (0,0.65);
\end{tikzpicture} & $\equiv$ &
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85, baseline=(c.base)]
  \draw (1.0,0) to[american current source, l=$i_{s,eq}$] (1.0,1.5);
  \draw (0,1.5) -- (1.0,1.5); \draw (0,0) -- (1.0,0); \abterm{0}{1.5}{left}
  \coordinate (c) at (0,0.65);
\end{tikzpicture} &
$i_{s,eq} = i_{s1} + i_{s2} + \dots + i_{sk}$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{minipage}

With conductances $G_j = 1/R_j$: $\Icol{i} = G_1\Vcol{v} + \dots + G_k\Vcol{v} =
G_{eq}\Vcol{v}$, so $G_{eq} = G_1 + \dots + G_k$. The share of $R_j$ is
$\Icol{i_j} = (G_j/G_{eq})\,\Icol{i}$ (\textbf{current divider}). Two resistors:
$R_{eq} = R_1R_2/(R_1 + R_2)$.

\begin{cautionbox}[Two connections that are not allowed]
Two voltage sources with $v_{s1} \ne v_{s2}$ in parallel break KVL. Two current sources with
$i_{s1} \ne i_{s2}$ in series break KCL. With equal values the connection is allowed, but the
current shared by the two voltage sources (or the voltage shared by the two current sources)
is not fixed.
\end{cautionbox}

\section{One source and one resistor}

Take a voltage source with a series resistor, and a current source with a parallel resistor.
In both, $\Icol{i}$ enters at $a$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c@{\hspace{14mm}}c@{}}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.75]
  \vsR{$v_s$}{$R_1$} \stport
\end{tikzpicture} &
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.75]
  \isR{$i_s$}{$R_2$} \stport
\end{tikzpicture}\\[2pt]
$\Vcol{v} = R_1\Icol{i} + v_s \qquad (1)$ &
$\Vcol{v} = R_2(\Icol{i} + i_s) = R_2\Icol{i} + R_2 i_s \qquad (2)$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In (2), $\Icol{i}$ and $i_s$ both flow into the top node, so $R_2$ carries $\Icol{i} + i_s$.
Both characteristics are straight lines in the $i$--$v$ plane. They are the same line when the
slopes and the intercepts agree.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.5\linewidth}
\begin{ideabox}[Source transformation]
The two one-ports are equivalent if
\[
  R_1 = R_2 = R \qquad\text{and}\qquad v_s = R\, i_s .
\]
Open circuit ($\Icol{i} = 0$): $\Vcol{v} = v_s$. Short circuit ($\Vcol{v} = 0$):
$\Icol{i} = -i_s$.
\end{ideabox}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\linewidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.75]
  \draw[-{Stealth[length=2mm]}] (-2.6,0) -- (1.6,0) node[right] {$\Icol{i}$};
  \draw[-{Stealth[length=2mm]}] (0,-0.5) -- (0,2.4) node[above] {$\Vcol{v}$};
  \draw[metured, very thick] (-2.3,-0.3) -- (1.3,2.1);
  \fill[metured] (0,1.233) circle (2pt); \node[font=\small] at (0.38,0.98) {$v_s$};
  \fill[metured] (-1.85,0) circle (2pt) node[below=2pt, font=\small] {$-i_s$};
  \node[font=\small, text=metured, anchor=west] at (1.0,1.15) {slope $R$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\begin{examplebox}[Example 10.4 --- a source transformation]
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.58\linewidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.72, baseline=(c.base)]
  \vsR{$20\V$}{$10\ohm$} \coordinate (c) at (0,0.8);
\end{tikzpicture}
\ $\equiv$\
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.72, baseline=(c.base)]

\begin{minipage}[c]{0.4\linewidth}
$R = 10\ohm$ and $i_s = v_s/R = 20/10 = 2\A$. Both have open-circuit voltage $20\V$ and
short-circuit current $2\A$ (out of $a$).
\end{minipage}
\end{examplebox}

\section{Solving LTI resistive circuits}

Series and parallel connections and source transformations simplify many circuits, but not
all. Two methods write the equations of the circuit as it is, without transforming it, and
work for every LTI resistive circuit:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{node voltage analysis} --- the $n - 1$ node voltages are the unknowns;
  \item \textbf{mesh current analysis} --- the $b - n + 1$ mesh currents are the unknowns.
\end{enumerate}

%==============================================================
\section*{Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{resistive} element has an algebraic law with no memory; a \textbf{dynamic}
        one ($L$, $C$) has a derivative. In an LTI resistive circuit every element, apart from
        the independent sources (inputs), is linear, time-invariant and resistive.
  \item \textbf{Substitution:} a part may be replaced by a source equal to its port voltage
        or current; nothing changes in the rest (if the solution stays unique).
  \item Two one-ports are \textbf{equivalent} if they have the same $i$--$v$ characteristic;
        the rest of the circuit cannot tell them apart.
  \item Series: $R_{eq} = \sum R_j$, $v_{s,eq} = \sum v_{sj}$. Parallel: $G_{eq} = \sum G_j$,
        $i_{s,eq} = \sum i_{sj}$. Source transformation: $v_s$ with series $R$ $\equiv$
        $i_s = v_s/R$ with parallel $R$.
\end{itemize}
